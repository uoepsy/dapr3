overview

  • problem: too many variables
  • solution: reduce
  • how?: pca/efa

starting point

  • measurement is difficult, and often introduces error.

  • especially in psychology, we might use “multi-item measures” (n different “tests” of the same thing)

  • necessary because:
    • constructs are multi-faceted
    • individual tests have noise/error

therefore…

  • increased “dimensionality”

  • more columns in our data, but they all kind of mean the same thing.

example context

biscuits!

construct: how much people love biscuits


measurement instrument:

  • digestives
  • hobnobs
  • rich tea
  • oreos
  • bourbon
  • choc-leibniz

for each Q, people rate it 1-7 (dislike <—> like)

PCA and EFA

PCA & EFA are about reducing to new dimensions without losing too much

  • the only real differences:
    • conceptual: is the dimension a “common cause” (EFA) or a “summary composite” (PCA)
      • if a “common cause” then we need it to be theoretically meaningful
    • practical: do we want to let these new dimensions be correlated? (EFA, rotated)

scenario 1 - EFA

  • if there’s just 1 thing driving ‘why people score high/low on these questions’?, and everything else is specific preferences for specific biscuit types, unrelated to one another… then:

  • the 1-factor model will look best

  • if we try to add more factors, we just end up with factors that have only 1 item.

    • this isn’t helpful - these factors don’t give us something ‘shared’. they just give us the hobnob-specific variation. we’re not reducing anything.
myefa1A <- fa(scenario_1[,-1], nfactors=1)

where do the rotations come in?

  • the point of rotations is to make a cleaner “simple structure”
    • items loading on to one factor and not onto others
  • the meaning of the factors are defined by their loadings, so this changes their meanings.
  • oblique rotations allow factors to correlate, which might be desirable if we think that to be the case

oblique/orthogonal

  • when 2 factors correlate, their combination explains variance in the data.

  • if we ask for uncorrelated dimensions, (orthogonal/no rotation) this variance ends up in the loadings

    • more cross loadings >> harder to define the factors
