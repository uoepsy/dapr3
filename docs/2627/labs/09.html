Quality Street

Our exercises today are concerning the factor structure of the “WHOQOL-BREF” - a brief measure of quality of life developed by the World Health Organisation (WHO). It contains 26 questions (the first two of which are treated individually as general single item measures). The remaining 24 questions captures four related aspects of quality of life: Physical, Psychological, Social and Environmental.

In practice, these four domain scores are often highly correlated (\(r > .70\)). Researcher A is curious about whether this scale is genuinely measuring four distinct domains or simply reflecting one underlying construct (a “General Quality of Life”). She wants to test whether a complex 4-factor structure is strictly necessary to explain the responses, or whether a simpler 1-factor model is sufficient. She collects WHOQOL-BREF data from 300 adults aged 25 to 55.
The wordings of the items can be found below.
The dataset can be accessed at https://uoepsy.github.io/data/whoqolbref-sampleA.csv

The loadings will be different.

  • without std.lv = TRUE, the first loading is fixed to 1, and all the others are in comparison to that one
  • with std.lv = TRUE, the loadings are all freely estimated but the latent variable has a variance of 1

For instance, the items loading on the social domain have these loadings when we use std.lv = TRUE: 0.576, 0.489, 0.510.
And when we don’t use it, we get: 1.000, 0.849, 0.885.

To see how they match up:

c(0.576, 0.489, 0.510) / 0.576
[1] 1.000 0.849 0.885

The factor covariances are also different.

  • without std.lv = TRUE, they are covariances between latent variables that have scales fixed to the same as their first item. covariances are dependent on units, remember!
  • with std.lv = TRUE, the latent variables have variances of 1, so they are all standardised. so covariances become correlations!

Question 5

How do the individual items look? How much variance in each item is explained by its corresponding factor?

Bonus Challenge: Can you calculate the “average variance explained” for each factor?

TipHints

When we have a standardised factor loading, it is just like EFA, where squaring it gives the variance in an item explained by a factor.

I love making plots rather than looking at numbers, so here we go!

standardizedsolution(fit_who) |>
  filter(op == "=~") |>
  mutate(r2 = est.std^2) |>
  group_by(lhs) |>
  mutate(mean_r2 = mean(r2)) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = rhs, y = r2)) +
  geom_col() +
  geom_hline(aes(yintercept = mean_r2),
             linetype = "dashed", color = "red") +
  ylim(0,1)+
  coord_flip() + 
  facet_wrap(~lhs, scales = "free_y")

Quality over Quantity

Specifically, he wants to ask if the optimal factor structure identified in younger adults (25–55) holds true in older adults aged 65+? To test this, Researcher B recruits a second sample of 300 adults aged 65 and above who complete the same 26 items.
